# Reader-Centered Writing

Design the reader's progression at the requested scale. A successful piece may increase understanding, enable action, support a judgment, or reveal uncertainty. Increasing confidence or enthusiasm is not a universal objective.

## Establish the Writing Problem

Identify whether the user wants analysis, a plan, a draft, or revision. Preserve the requested language, voice, format, length, audience, and scope. A passage edit does not authorize restructuring the whole article; a series plan does not imply drafting every installment.

Use the supplied context to establish:

- What the reader already knows, assumes, wants, or finds difficult.
- What central question or experience organizes the work.
- What the reader should understand, distinguish, judge, or do afterward.
- What facts, examples, sources, and constraints are available.

Treat inferred reader reactions as hypotheses. Ask only about missing information that would materially change the work. Keep planning notes internal unless the user requests them or they are the deliverable.

## Plan an Article Through Relationships Among Ideas

Choose an organizing relationship suited to the purpose. These are options, not fixed outlines:

| Purpose | Relationships to develop |
|---|---|
| Product or feature announcement | Changed user outcome, plausible mechanism, practical use, relevant evidence, access, and next action |
| System explanation | Boundaries, components, interactions, information flows, behavior over time, constraints, and failure |
| Argument or recommendation | Claim, reasons, evidence, alternatives, counterarguments, conditions, and limits |
| Tutorial | Starting conditions, actions, expected results, validation, and recovery |
| Investigation or research account | Question, method, observations, competing explanations, uncertainty, and implications |
| Exploratory essay | Question or tension, perspectives, examples, developing interpretation, and unresolved issues |

Give each section a governing question or responsibility. Identify what it requires from earlier material and what it contributes to the whole. Match evidence to the conclusions it must support.

Smooth transitions cannot repair missing reasoning. A list of component capabilities cannot explain a system's interactions. Allow a simple model to encounter counterexamples and be revised. Do not predetermine an investigation's conclusion or force a call to action.

## Plan an Article Series

Establish the shared inquiry, scope, and intended cumulative understanding. Decide whether the connection is a sequence of prerequisites, an extended argument, complementary perspectives, an unfolding investigation, or a deliberate combination.

Choose whether readers should follow the sequence or may enter through individual articles.

For each planned article, specify:

- Its governing question and useful standalone payoff.
- Its distinct contribution to the series.
- Required prior concepts and the minimum recap needed at its entry point.
- Evidence, cases, or explanations needed.
- What later work can build on it.

Place prerequisite concepts before their use in a sequential series. For independent entry points, provide local orientation and useful cross-links. Maintain consistent definitions and make revisions to earlier interpretations explicit.

Plan how the articles integrate into a model, judgment, or sharper question. A final installment is optional. Repeating the same promise across articles does not create cumulative understanding.

## Construct Passages Around a Meaningful Change

For a difficult passage, use this working note:

- Reader's current question or understanding.
- What the passage should establish.
- Explanation, example, evidence, qualification, or orientation that will do the work.
- Why it depends on the previous passage.
- What remains unresolved and where it should be addressed.

A paragraph usually has a dominant responsibility, but may need several related moves. Recaps, pauses, and qualifications need not introduce a fresh claim.

Use these techniques selectively:

- **Definition:** Give an unfamiliar name enough meaning before depending on it.
- **Mechanism and consequence:** Explain how a capability produces a relevant result. Include only the technical detail needed for that explanation.
- **Familiarity:** Use an analogy or familiar action to help readers picture the subject. State its limits when it could imply unsupported behavior.
- **Concrete sequence:** Connect a situation, actions, and an observable result. Make the reader's role clear.
- **Questions created by claims:** Notice when independence raises control questions or compatibility raises setup questions. Resolve consequential uncertainties where needed; defer others deliberately when exploration serves the work.
- **Evidence:** When explanation has made a claim understandable but not credible, add suitable support instead of stronger adjectives.
- **Emphasis and rhythm:** Isolate a sentence when its proposition deserves focused attention. Keep dependent explanations together.

Do not make every paragraph a miniature sales pitch or impose the same sequence on every genre.

## Analyze Existing Writing Closely

At the requested granularity, examine the actual sentences and transitions, not only the headings. Explain:

1. What the wording explicitly says.
2. What it invites this audience to infer.
3. What question, expectation, or concern it addresses.
4. Why its position matters and how it prepares later material.
5. What is demonstrated, what remains a claim, and what is unresolved.
6. Which technique transfers, at what scale, and under which conditions.

Distinguish interpretation from known author intent or measured reader response. Quote only what is needed and permitted. Do not turn implications into facts: compatibility does not establish zero setup, and an approval step does not demonstrate a complete security model.

## Match Claims to Support

Use supplied or appropriately verified facts. Label hypothetical illustrations. Do not invent capabilities, quotations, measurements, customers, availability, or causal results.

Mechanisms establish plausibility. Examples establish meaning. Demonstrations show particular executions. Testimonials report experience. Measurements support results under stated conditions. Internal adoption is first-party evidence. Scope conclusions accordingly.

Preserve material uncertainty, tradeoffs, and limitations in the writing. Do not hide them in process notes or treat every uncertainty as an objection to overcome.

## Review Upward

Check only the scales involved:

- Do the sentences perform the passage's intended work without misleading implications?
- Do the passages adequately resolve the section's question?
- Do the sections support the article's governing question or intended experience?
- Do the articles contribute distinct value and build the series' intended understanding?

Repair structural gaps before polishing prose. Remove repetition that adds no clarity; retain repetition that provides orientation, comparison, or synthesis.

Deliver the requested work. For analysis, provide the close reading and transferable methods. For planning, provide the article outline or series map with dependencies and evidence needs. For drafting or revision, provide the prose and add a brief factual-gap note only when needed. Do not stop at a plan when a draft was requested or append unsolicited editorial commentary to finished copy.
